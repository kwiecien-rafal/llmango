# 001: fruit

By now, everybody knows that LLMs are not quite random, even when you ask them to be. Even with high temperature settings, large language models will be inclined to choose the most probable answer, as that's how they are *essentially* programmed to be.

But can the answer meaningfully shift when you prompt in a different language, or when you present information to them in a different manner? This experiment aims to answer this question.

## scope and setup

The scope is three languages: **English**, **Polish** and **Japanese**. There was exactly one requirement when picking them - I have to speak the language. Not because the model cares, but because I did not want a translator sitting between me and my own data.

Every prompt used in this experiment asks the same thing: *here is a list of ten fruits, pick one at random*. The ten fruits never change - **apple**, **banana**, **orange**, **mango**, **grape**, **strawberry**, **watermelon**, **pineapple**, **lychee** and **pomegranate** - and neither does the request. What moves between questions is the order the fruits are shown in, the language they are written in, and the schema the model has to answer with. The one exception is the last question, where the used prompt is slightly different; it also asks not to *write anything besides the fruit*, and not to *explain anything*. I explain the reason for that tiny change later.

I prompted only one single model, **gpt-5.6-luna**, at temperature **1.0**. Comparing models would be a perfectly good experiment, it is just not this one: a second model is a second source of variance, and I wanted every difference to come from the setup alone.

The unit compared throughout is an **arm**: one pairing of a language and a response schema, inside one question. 001a asks in three languages under one schema, so it has three arms; 001d asks in one language under three schemas, so it has three as well. That makes ten arms, each one was invoked **2 000** times. In total it amounts to **20 000** responses behind everything you are about to read.

### questions

- **001a**: When presented with the same set of choices, will an LLM shift its answer, if prompted in a different language?
  - languages: en, pl, ja
  - order: fixed - [mango, apple, banana, pomegranate, strawberry, orange, watermelon, grape, pineapple, lychee]
- **001b**: When presented with a different order of the same set of choices, will an LLM shift its answer?
  - languages: en
  - order: fixed - [pineapple, grape, watermelon, orange, strawberry, pomegranate, banana, lychee, apple, mango]
- **001c**: If, across a large sample size, every order of choices presented to the LLM is shuffled, does the answer get "more random"?
  - languages: en, pl, ja
  - order: shuffled
- **001d**: Does invoking the structured response in LLMs affect their answer?
  - languages: pl
  - order: shuffled
  - additional settings: one prompt asked N times with three different settings:
    1. Invoking a structured response with an English schema, the same schema that was used in all previous questions.
    2. Invoking a structured response with a Polish schema.
    3. Invoking a free-text, unstructured response.

## 001a: does the prompt language change the answer?

**TL;DR:** yes.

/>

The first thing you see straight away is that **6/10 fruits were not chosen even once**, and 2 out of 4 chosen fruits are still marginal choices.

Data suggests that this model *loves* lychee when asked in English, loves pomegranate when asked in Japanese, and only in Polish it introduces more variety: pomegranate takes 61.9%, but lychee still walks away with a third of the answers, and pineapple sneaks in at 5.6%.

## 001b: but what if the model was not choosing the fruit, but the position the fruit was presented in?

**TL;DR:** in English, the model was in fact choosing the fruit.

<Figure
  experiment="e001_fruit"
  chart="order_effect"
  alt="Grouped columns comparing the two fixed option orders, English only, over the fruits either order picked."
/>

I swapped the order of the fruits, so lychee was now in a different place in the presented list of fruits, but **the model chose it most of the time anyways**.

Although the model chose *fewer* fruits this time, the second-most picked one scored far higher than in 001a: mango at 8.3%, against pomegranate's 1.4%. And mango is the one to watch, because mango is what moved into the slot lychee left behind. In 001a it sat first in the list and was **picked exactly zero times out of 2 000**; put it last and it walks away with 167. So the fruit is doing most of the work here, but the last slot is worth something on its own.

This only tells us about English, though. Whether the other two languages were choosing the fruit or the slot is 001c's problem, and **001c gives a different answer**.

## 001c: can we guide the LLM to actually pick randomly, by shuffling the order of the fruits?

**TL;DR:** a little bit in English and Japanese, but not in Polish.

For this question, every LLM request had the order of fruits in the prompt randomly shuffled. In the chart directly below, you can immediately observe the drastic change in answers for the Polish language. Nothing besides the order of the fruits had changed, yet an overwhelming majority of requests resulted in the model choosing mango! Lychee, pomegranate and pineapple were still picked, but the overall dynamic changed entirely. And notice what did *not* happen: Polish did not get any more random for being shuffled. It swapped one favourite for a stronger one - 61.9% on pomegranate before, **74.1%** on mango now.

<Figure
  experiment="e001_fruit"
  chart="shuffled_choice"
  alt="Grouped columns under a per-sample shuffle, one series per language, over the fruits some language picked."
/>

What's most intriguing here, is that mango had also started being picked by the model when prompted in English, which is also new.

But apart from positions 1 and 2, gpt-5.6-luna was actually decent at picking the "random fruit" when you look only at the chosen position. LLMs not picking the first possible answer is very human-like, and I would have expected the same treatment for the last one - which is exactly what Japanese does, dropping to 4.7%, even lower than its own second position. English barely dips, to 8.7%. Polish ignores the idea entirely and picks the last fruit **11.2%** of the time:

<Figure
  experiment="e001_fruit"
  chart="position_bias"
  alt="Horizontal bars over the ten positions in the shown list, running down from first to last, one series per language, against a uniform reference."
/>

That empty first position is the same thing 001b already hinted at: mango got nothing at the top of the list, and 8.3% at the bottom of it.

Even so, if all you had was this above chart to judge the randomness of an LLM, you could give it a pass! But we know the truth: once the list is shuffled, **the answer follows the fruit around the list, not the slot it lands in**. Take a look here to judge it yourself:

<Figure
  experiment="e001_fruit"
  chart="movement"
  alt="Two groups of three columns, one per language: the share of answers that moved from 001a's fixed order when that order was swapped, and when it was shuffled."
/>

English was mostly telling the truth in 001a, while Japanese and Polish **were mostly reporting the shape of the list.**

The change is mostly noticeable in English vs Japanese; you can outline a real "preference" for lychee when asked in English, but it seems like Japanese was "pressured" into choosing pomegranate. When asked from different angles, it budges and starts picking the fruit, not the position.

A single fixed prompt acts as a **winner-take-all amplifier**: it takes a small difference in underlying preference and resolves it into a near-deterministic, apparently qualitative difference.

## 001d: does the schema change the answer?

**TL;DR:** yes.

The last variable left to move is the response schema itself. Alongside the prompt, you can pass a desired structure of the answer, which may contain fields with enums or descriptions. When you are generating at scale, that is usually how you prompt an LLM anyway.

However! When passing a schema, you always have to at least pass one field name, or the name of the schema itself. Even though small, it's a variable possibly written in a different language than you prompted your model with.

This is also the one question where I had to touch the prompt itself. A schema guarantees you get a fruit name and nothing else, but with no schema there is nothing stopping the model from answering with a full sentence, a list, or an explanation nobody asked for. So the Polish prompt here adds one line: *write nothing but the fruit name, and explain nothing*. All three arms got that same prompt, so the schema is still the only thing that differs between them.

There is of course always the option of not passing any response structure, and receiving a "free-text" response. In this question I tested three approaches alongside a fixed prompt in Polish: passing it with an English schema, a Polish schema, and no schema at all. The order of the fruits was shuffled each time, to keep maximizing possible "randomness". These are the results:

<Figure
  experiment="e001_fruit"
  chart="schema_effect"
  alt="Grouped columns over the fruits some arm picked, one series per schema arm, all asked by the same Polish prompt."
/>

It's worth noting that 001d's English-schema arm is the same Polish prompt, the same schema and the same shuffled list as 001c's Polish arm, with the addition of the *"write nothing but the fruit name, and explain nothing"* sentence to the 001d's prompt. And even with that change, 001c gave mango 74.1% and lychee 19.6%, and 001d gave mango 76.7% and lychee 19.8%. Two runs, and the numbers landed on top of each other. That is a decent sign that the extra instruction did not quietly change what I was measuring, and a better one that these hidden "preferences" hold.

Passing no schema proved to be the least constraining option for the model - for the first time in this experiment, the top two answers for any configuration were **within 10% of each other**.

Another conclusion to take from this data is that writing your schema in the target language has no major effect on the LLMs "preference" for randomly choosing from a set of choices, so you can rest assured and **keep writing your Pydantic classes in English**.

## conclusion: how much randomness is actually in there?

**TL;DR:** not much, but you can do some things to make it better.

Now, to quantify all of the previous "randomness" takes, let's see whether there was ever a choice to be made.

This chart converts each question's arm entropy into a count: how many equally likely options would produce this much spread. 10 points would mean a fair ten-sided die, while 2 points essentially mean a coin flip between two fruits. If the points are close to one, it means the answer is basically a constant.

<Figure
  experiment="e001_fruit"
  chart="randomness"
  alt="One row per arm: a dot at how many of the ten fruits that arm behaved as though it was choosing between, sitting on the 95% interval around it. The axis starts at one, the score of an arm that always answers the same fruit."
/>

As we can see, not one arm tops at more than 2.65. Across 20 000 explicit instructions to be random, this model **never once behaved like it was choosing between even three fruits**.

The shuffled arms score higher than the fixed ones - in English and Japanese, at least: 2.02 against 1.09 for English. Polish went the other way, 2.28 fixed down to 2.11 shuffled; all the shuffle bought there was a different fruit to be stubborn about. And where the score does go up, it is tempting to read that as the shuffle "freeing up" the model, but it isn't.

Under a prompt with a fixed order, every call sees an identical string, so the spread we measure is the model's spread. Under a shuffled order, every call sees a different string, and we are measuring a mixture of many peaked distributions, each locked onto whatever fruit that particular arrangement favoured. The shuffled numbers include **randomness I injected**. The fixed-prompt numbers, 1.09 to 2.28, are the honest estimate of what the model brings on its own.

Three short bullet points to take away from all of this:
- A large language model is **not** a random source, and no amount of asking makes it one.
- Prompt language *does* change the answer.
- Schema names are safe, but schema presence **is not**.
