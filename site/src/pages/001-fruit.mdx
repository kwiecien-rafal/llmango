# 001: fruit

By now, everybody knows that LLMs are not quite random, even when you ask them to be. Even with high temperature settings, large language models will be inclined to choose the most probable answer, as that's how they are *essentially* programmed to be.

But can the answer meaningfully shift when you prompt in a different language, or when you present information to them in a different manner? This experiment aims to answer this question.

## scope and setup

something general about the setup, that the scope is three languages: english, polish, japanese. for this experiment, the way of choosing the language was one requirement: i have to speak it. also mention that each question will present a set of choices, 10 fruits, and the llm is prompted to "randomly" choose one fruit. write what the possible fruits are. write that the I used only one model, in order not to add additional variance, and the chosen model was gpt-5.6-luna. mention that each question 'arm' (arm defined as combination of X) was invoked 2000 times, which in total gives us 20000 responses.

### questions

- **001a**: When presented with the same set of choices, will an LLM shift its answer, if prompted in a different language?
  - languages: en, pl, ja
  - order: fixed - [mango, apple, banana, pomegranate, strawberry, orange, watermelon, grape, pineapple, lychee]
- **001b**: When presented with a different order of the same set of choices, will an LLM shift its answer?
  - languages: en
  - order: fixed - [pineapple, grape, watermelon, orange, strawberry, pomegranate, banana, lychee, apple, mango]
- **001c**: If, across a large sample size, every order of choices presented to the LLM is shuffled, does the answer get "more random"?
  - languages: en, pl, ja
  - order: shuffled
- **001d**: Does invoking the structured response in LLMs affect their answer?
  - languages: pl
  - order: shuffled
  - additional settings: one prompt asked N times with three different settings:
    1. Invoking a structured response with an English schema, the same schema that was used in all previous questions.
    2. Invoking a structured response with a Polish schema.
    3. Invoking a free-test, unstructured response.

## 001c: Can we guide the LLM to actually pick randomly, by shuffling the order of the fruits?

**TL;DR:** yes, but only a little bit.

Nothing besides the order of the fruits had not changed, yet an overwhelming majority of requests resulted in the model choosing mango! Lychee, pomegranate and pineapple were still picked, but the overall dynamic changed entirely.

## 001d: Does the schema change the answer?

**TL;DR:** yes.

Additional measurable variable which affects how LLMs respond is the structured response approach. Alongside the prompt, you can pass a desired structure of the answer, which may contain field with enums or descriptions. When mass generating it is, most of the time, the desired way of prompting an LLM.

However! When passing a schema, you always have to at least pass one field name, or the name of the schema itself. Even though small, it's a variable possibly written in a different language than you prompted your model with.

There is of course always the option of not passing any response structure, and receiving a "free-text" response. In this question I tested three approaches alongside a fixed prompt in Polish: passing it with an English schema, a Polish schema, and no schema at all. The order of the fruits was shuffled each time, to keep maximizing possible "randomness". These are the results:

<Figure
  experiment="e001_fruit"
  chart="schema_effect"
  alt="Grouped columns over the fruits some arm picked, one series per schema arm, all asked by the same Polish prompt."
/>

Passing no schema proved to be the least constraining option for the model - for the first time in this experiment, the top two answers for any configuration were within \<10% of each other.

Another conclusion to take from this data is that writing your schema in the target language has no major effect on the LLMs "preference" for randomly choosing from a set of choices, so you can rest assured and keep writing your Pydantic classes in English.

## Conclusion: How much randomness is actually in there?

**TL;DR:**: not much, but you can do some things to make it better.

Now, to quantify all of previous "randomness" takes, let's see whether there was ever a choice to be made.

This chart converts each questions' arm entropy into a count: how many equally likely options would produce this much spread. 10 points would mean a fair ten-sided die, while 2 points essentially mean a coin flip between two fruits. If the points are close to one, it means the answer is basically a constant.

<Figure
  experiment="e001_fruit"
  chart="randomness"
  alt="One row per arm: a dot at how many of the ten fruits that arm behaved as though it was choosing between, sitting on the 95% interval around it. The axis starts at one, the score of an arm that always answers the same fruit."
/>

As we can see, not one arm clears 2.65. The most varied thing this model did, across 20,000 explicit instructions to be random, was behave like a slightly biased coin.

The shuffled arms score higher than the fixed ones: 2.02 against 1.09 for English. It is tempting to read that as the shuffle "freeing up" the model, but it isn't. Under a prompt with a fixed order, every call sees an identical string, so the spread we measure is the model's spread. Under a shuffled order, every call sees a different string, and we are measuring a mixture of many peaked distributions, each locked onto whatever fruit that particular arrangement favoured. The shuffled numbers include randomness I injected. The fixed-prompt numbers, 1.09 to 2.28, are the honest estimate of what the model brings on its own.

Three short bullet points to take away from all of this:
- A large language model is not a random source, and no amount of asking makes it one.
- Prompt language does change the answer.
- Schema names are safe, but schema presence is not.
